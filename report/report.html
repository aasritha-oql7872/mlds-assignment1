2. Value and policy iteration: shuttle dispatching (Part 2)

2.1 The MDP

ComponentDefinition
States in {0,...,200}: customers waiting at the start of a decision epoch (capped at 200 as the assignment allows).
Actiona in {0 = hold, 1 = dispatch}.
DispatchA dispatched shuttle takes away min(s, K) customers, K = 15. Customers left behind: post = s − a·min(s, K).
Costc(s,a) = cf·a + ch·post, with cf = 100 and ch = 2 (holding cost is charged on customers left waiting after the dispatch decision, as the assignment states).
TransitionThen A ~ Unif{1,2,3,4,5} customers arrive: s' = min(post + A, 200).
ObjectiveMinimise expected discounted cost, γ = 0.95. (Equivalent to maximising reward = −cost.)

Assumptions stated. (1) Customers who arrive when the station already holds 200 leave (balk), so the cap is a hard ceiling and no probability mass is lost. (2) Arrivals happen after the dispatch decision within a period, so a dispatch always acts on customers already waiting. (3) The problem is solved as cost minimisation. (4) Backward induction uses terminal value VT = 0 at T = 500.

2.2 The three solvers

  • (a) Enumeration (backward induction), T = 500. V500 = 0; for t = 499,...,0: Vt(s) = mina [ c(s,a) + γ Σs' P(s'|s,a) Vt+1(s') ]. Plot V0.
  • (b) Value iteration, T = ∞. Iterate the same Bellman update from V = 0 until ||Vk+1 − Vk||∞ < ε(1−γ)/(2γ) with ε = 10−8, which guarantees an ε-optimal greedy policy. It stopped after 500 iterations.
  • (c) Policy iteration. Exact policy evaluation by solving the linear system (I − γPπ)V = cπ, then greedy improvement, with ties keeping the previous action so the loop must terminate. It converged in 6 rounds (states whose action changed per round: 196, 28, 22, 7, 1, 0).

2.7 Given an additional 3 months, what other validation would I do? (Part 2)

  • Check the modelling assumptions, not only the solvers. Re-solve with other conventions (holding cost on customers before dispatch, arrivals before the decision, customers queueing instead of balking at the cap) and report how much the threshold of 13 moves.
  • Sensitivity to the arrival distribution. Estimate arrivals from real data (non-uniform, time-of-day dependent) and test how a policy optimal for Unif{1..5} performs under the true process; add a robust or Bayesian variant.
  • Larger capacity and cap. Vary K and the 200 cap, and a multi-shuttle or multi-stop version, to confirm that the threshold structure persists and that the cap never binds under the optimal policy.
  • Independent implementations. Cross-check against a second implementation (for example a linear-programming formulation of the MDP) and against an off-the-shelf MDP toolbox.
  • Statistical depth. More Monte Carlo seeds with confidence intervals, and a check of the discount-factor trade-off (γ → 1 average-cost limit).

3. Policy gradient, Part 1: REINFORCE (CartPole-v1 and Pong-v5)

3.1 Method

The policy πθ(a|s) is a one-hidden-layer ReLU network with a softmax output. After each batch of N episodes we take one Adam step on the loss

L(θ) = − (1/N) Σepisodes Σt log πθ(at|st) · Gt,   Gt = rt + γ Gt+1,

so −∇L is the reward-to-go REINFORCE estimate of the policy gradient of the discounted return J = E[Σt γt rt] (with the usual convention described in the note below). Using the return from step t onward (rather than from the start of the episode) is valid because later actions cannot change earlier rewards, and it removes noise.

Learning rate. The assignment suggests 0.01 and allows another value with justification. For CartPole I used 0.003 because 0.01 sometimes collapses a run to a near-random policy (one of five seeds ended at an evaluation mean of 9 with the baseline, another at 16 without it; see Table 3). Pong uses 0.001 with larger batches, since one update there averages about 50,000 decisions.

Pong-specific choices.

  • Difference frames. One frame carries no motion information; subtracting the previous frame shows the ball's direction. The first frame of an episode has no predecessor, so its input is all zeros.
  • Returns restart at every point. Each +1 / −1 ends a rally and the ball is re-served. With γ = 0.99 an action would otherwise be credited or blamed for the outcome of unrelated later rallies. This sharpens credit assignment. Training and evaluation scores in the figures are still the raw game score (−21 to +21).
  • No 1000-step episode cap. A Pong game lasts about 1,000 to 5,000 agent steps once the agent is competent, so a 1000-step cap would truncate games in the middle. CartPole's own 500-step limit is treated as terminal.
  • Environment library. gymnasium + ale-py (ALE/Pong-v5, frame-skip 4, sticky actions 0.25) instead of gym[atari], which needs a separate ROM licence step. The assignment requires Pong-v5, which is this version.

A note on discounting. The lecture notes weight each reward in the step-t term by γt'−t (reward-to-go measured from t), which is what is implemented here. The Lab 4 formula uses γt' with the absolute step index, which multiplies the step-t term by an extra γt. That extra factor is what the exact gradient of the discounted return contains; dropping it, as the lecture form does, weights late steps of an episode more heavily and gives every step the same effective horizon. I used the lecture form, which is also the common practical choice.

Evaluation. After training, 500 episodes are rolled out with the trained stochastic policy, on seeds never used in training. The histogram, mean and standard deviation (sample, ddof=1) are reported.

3.6 Given an additional 3 months, what other validation would I do? (Part 1)

  • Many seeds with confidence intervals. The headline curves are one training seed each (Pong costs about 11 to 14 hours per run). I would run at least 10 seeds per setting, report mean ± confidence interval, and test differences formally.
  • Hyper-parameter sweeps over learning rate, batch size, hidden width and discount, with the budget held fixed, to show the choices are not tuned to one lucky seed.
  • Pong ablations of the design choices: no frame difference (single frame), returns not restarted at each point, a 1000-step cap, and sticky actions on versus off.
  • Policy quality beyond the average score: win rate against the opponent, longest rally, and evaluation with a greedy (arg-max) policy as well as the stochastic one.
  • Stability checks: longer training to see whether the CartPole dips seen in Figure 7 persist, entropy and gradient-norm tracking, and gradient clipping.
  • Cross-checking against a reference implementation (for example a library policy-gradient baseline) on the same environments.

4. Policy gradient, Part 2: REINFORCE with a baseline

4.1 Baseline chosen and how it is implemented

I use a learned, state-dependent baseline: a second network Vφ(s) with the same shape as the policy network but one output (4 → 128 → 1 for CartPole, 6400 → 200 → 1 for Pong), trained by mean-squared error to predict the reward-to-go, with its own Adam optimiser at the same learning rate as the policy. The policy gradient then uses the advantage:

∇J ≈ (1/N) Σepisodes Σt ∇ log πθ(at|st) · ( Gt − Vφ(st) )

The only difference from Part 1 in the code is one line (adv = G - v.detach() instead of adv = G), plus the value-network update. The baseline's value is detached so the policy gradient does not flow into Vφ.

Why this keeps the gradient unbiased. Vφ(st) depends only on the state, not on the action taken, and Ea~π[∇ log π(a|s)] = 0, so subtracting it adds zero in expectation while removing a state-dependent offset from Gt. Why a state-dependent baseline rather than a constant: in CartPole every reward is +1, so every return is positive and a constant baseline can only remove a global average. V(s) captures how good the current state is (for CartPole, how far from failing), so the advantage G − V(s) says whether this action did better or worse than usual from here. The lecture's constant baseline and the optimal B(s) = Vπ(s) are the two ends of this choice; this is the second. All other settings (γ, learning rate, batch size, number of episodes, seeds) are identical to Part 1.

What the evidence says, and what it does not.

  • Gradient variance is lower with the baseline. On CartPole the variability of the gradient norm is roughly three times lower during most of training (Figure 17, right); measured at a fixed policy, the per-episode gradient variance falls by 5.2× (Section 4.6), and in the exact-gradient test by 201×.
  • Faster early learning. On Pong the baseline run climbs out of the random-play level sooner (it reaches a 100-episode average of −5 after about 1,800 episodes, against about 2,600 without it; Figure 18). On CartPole its curve is above plain REINFORCE between about episodes 200 and 700 (Figure 17).
  • Final scores are about the same, and on Pong the baseline is slightly lower. The CartPole runs tie (485.6 vs 487.5) and on Pong Part 1 ends about one point higher (13.28 vs 12.23). Each is one training seed, so this difference should not be over-read; the evaluation standard error (about 0.17 on Pong) only reflects evaluation noise, not the seed-to-seed spread in training.
  • Over 5 CartPole seeds (Table 3) the baseline gives a higher average last-100 training reward (321.5 vs 223.6 at lr 0.003; 241.6 vs 85.2 at lr 0.01), and the per-seed spread is large in both groups. The evaluation means of the final policies (251 vs 209) are not statistically distinguishable with five seeds (a Welch t statistic of about 0.5).
  • Plausible reasons the baseline helps less on Pong (hypotheses, not tested here): returns already restart at each point and stay between about −1 and +1, so there is less variance to remove; each update averages ten long episodes; and a 6400-input value network is slower to fit than the policy.

Both methods also show the instability typical of vanilla policy gradients on CartPole: the 100-episode average can drop sharply after reaching 400+ (Figures 7, 13, 17), which is why a single curve should not be read as a reliable ranking.

4.7 Given an additional 3 months, what other validation would I do? (Part 2)

  • Compare baselines properly, with equal tuning effort: no baseline, a constant (running-mean) baseline, the lecture's optimal constant baseline (returns weighted by squared gradient norm), the learned V(s), and generalised advantage estimation, on at least 10 seeds each, with confidence intervals.
  • Measure variance directly during training, at many checkpoints along the run and not only at one fixed policy, and check that its reduction translates into faster or more reliable learning.
  • Baseline quality: track the value network's explained variance, sweep its learning rate and number of updates per batch, and test a shared-trunk network.
  • Pong without the reset-per-point trick, where the returns are larger and the baseline should matter more, to test the hypothesis above.
  • Normalising advantages and adding an entropy bonus, which commonly stabilise policy gradients, and checking whether they remove the dips in the CartPole curves.

5. Summary

  • Shuttle MDP: enumeration, value iteration and policy iteration agree: dispatch iff at least 13 customers are waiting; V*(0) = 576.67. 41 of 41 checks pass.
  • REINFORCE: CartPole is solved (mean 487.5 of a maximum 500); Pong is learned to a mean of +13.28 against −20.35 for random play. 20 of 20 learning checks and 23 of 23 edge-case checks pass.
  • Baseline: reduces gradient variance (5.2× on CartPole, 201× in the exact test) and speeds up early learning, but at the budgets used it does not clearly improve the final score on either game.
